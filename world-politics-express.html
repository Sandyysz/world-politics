Station 01 — Wakanda
Sovereignty

The right to

Black Panther (2018)

Wakanda sits on the world's largest deposit of vibranium — the most valuable resource on Earth — yet for centuries it chooses to hide, presenting itself as a third-world farming nation. This is the sovereignty paradox made cinematic: the absolute right of a state to determine its own fate, even when the world demands otherwise.

The film's central conflict — T'Challa's conservatism versus Killmonger's internationalism — maps almost perfectly onto the debate between sovereignty-first non-interventionism and the Responsibility to Protect doctrine.

IR Concept

Westphalian sovereignty (1648) establishes that states have supreme authority within their own borders — no outside interference. Wakanda's arc, from total isolationism to global engagement, dramatises the real tension between this principle and the modern Responsibility to Protect doctrine, which argues sovereignty is conditional on how a state treats its people.

The Diplomat, Netflix (2023)

Kate Wyler is the new U.S. Ambassador to the UK. Within days she is navigating a potential act of war, a marriage used as political theatre, and a web of alliances where nothing can be said directly. The Diplomat strips diplomacy down to its mechanics — the gap between public posture and private negotiation.

Joseph Nye's concept of soft power runs through every scene: the US does not always threaten or bribe — it persuades, attracts, co-opts. The series shows how much of that persuasion happens in rooms no camera ever enters.

IR Concept

Diplomacy operates on two tracks simultaneously. Track I is official state-to-state communication; Track II is the back-channel — academics, NGOs, informal envoys. Joseph Nye's soft power framework argues that a state's most durable influence comes not from what it can force, but from what others willingly adopt. The Diplomat dramatises how these two tracks collide in real time during a crisis.

Barbie (2023)

Barbieland is a world built entirely on rules about who leads, who follows, and what power looks like. Those rules feel natural to everyone inside — because they always have been that way. When Barbie crosses into the Real World, she discovers what feminist IR theorists have argued for decades: the rules were never natural. They were constructed.

The film's genius is the flip — a matriarchy so total it becomes a mirror held up to patriarchy. Both are equally arbitrary. Both feel inevitable from within.

IR Concept

Judith Butler's concept of gender performativity argues that gender is not something we are born with, but something we perform — repeated acts that produce the appearance of a natural identity. Feminist IR theory extends this: global institutions (the UN Security Council, NATO, the IMF) were built by and for a particular gendered subject. Barbieland makes the invisible architecture of gender visible by simply flipping it.

District 9 (2009)

A million extraterrestrials arrive over Johannesburg. Malnourished, leaderless, they are given a camp — not asylum, not citizenship, not rights. Twenty years later the camp is a slum under military control, and the government is planning forced relocation. District 9 renders the refugee experience with unflinching clarity: the bureaucracy of statelessness, the dehumanisation of the displaced, the violence of borders as political tools.

The aliens — called "prawns" — are the perfect allegorical mirror for every refugee population told their presence is a problem to be managed, not a humanity to be recognised.

IR Concept

The 1951 Refugee Convention established the right to seek asylum and the principle of non-refoulement — states cannot return refugees to a place where they face serious harm. District 9 shows a future (and a present) where that legal framework has been hollowed out: camps that become permanent, bureaucratic non-personhood, and the systematic dehumanisation that makes violence against the displaced politically acceptable.
